## Governance is just operations

There is no separate governance engine, no consensus round, and **no quorum**. The access-control, membership, and admin planes of [the operation model](/protocol/operations/) are governance — they are operations in a governance scope, folded by the same last-writer-wins projection, summarized by the same scope root. A membership change converges exactly the way a data write does.

What this chapter adds is _meaning_: what those governance operations say, the structure they maintain, and how the system decides whether an author was allowed to write one.

## The hierarchy

Governance scopes form a tree (the scope tree from [Concepts & Scopes](/protocol/concepts/)):

- A **namespace** is the root governance scope — a group with no parent. It anchors identity, admin, and policy for everything beneath it.
- **Subgroups** nest under it, each its own scope with its own key and members.
- **Contexts** (the running applications) attach to a group; a context's members are the group's members.

A subgroup is either **Open** or **Restricted**. In an Open subgroup, a parent member who holds the "join open subgroups" capability is a member by _inheritance_ — no explicit operation needed. A Restricted subgroup is closed: membership requires a direct, deliberate admission. Because membership only ever narrows as you descend, pointing an operation "up" the tree never leaks visibility — the property [Concepts & Scopes](/protocol/concepts/) relied on.

## The founder

A scope tree has to start somewhere, and that start can't appeal to a prior admin — there isn't one yet. So a namespace begins with a single **genesis operation** that names its **founder**. It is **self-authorizing**: its only checks are that it is signed by the founder it names and that it has no parents (it is genuinely the first operation). From it, the founder is recorded as admin and owner, and every node that later replays the DAG derives the same founder — authority is established by a replayable operation, not by out-of-band trust.

### The id commits to the founder

A self-authorizing genesis has one weakness on its own: nothing in a random namespace id says *which* founder is the real one, so a peer could sign a genesis naming itself for an id it did not create, and a fresh replica syncing only from that peer would believe it. So **every** namespace id is **derived from its founder**: `namespace_id = domain_hash("calimero.namespace.id.v1", [founder_account_id, salt])`, with a 32-byte salt that lets one account found many namespaces. The genesis carries the salt (`NamespaceCreatedV2`, the only genesis there is), and every replica refuses a genesis whose founder and salt do not reproduce the id.

That makes a forged genesis impossible rather than merely detectable. A genesis must be signed by the founder it names, and its id must derive from that founder, so a self-consistent forgery can only found ids its own signer's account hashes to — claiming somebody else's would mean finding a SHA-256 preimage. This holds on a bare replica syncing from a hostile peer, the one case the "already established" check cannot protect. Nothing founds a namespace any other way: there is no genesis without a salt, and no caller can pick a namespace id — only, at most, the salt it is derived with.

The same pair is proof of founding for anyone outside the namespace. Every replica keeps it, and the admin API returns it as `founding { founderAccountId, salt }` on the namespace, so a service shown both can confirm which account founded a namespace without holding any of its governance state. The salt cannot be replayed for another account.

An ordinary namespace draws its salt at random. The **account namespace** — the one every device of an account follows — derives it instead from the account root's secret, under its own domain separator, and its founder is the account itself. Every device holding the root computes the same id, and nobody holding only the account id can: the salt reveals nothing about the secret, and it is carried only by that namespace's genesis, which only its own members hold.

## Membership: joining

Joining is a two-part dance, because a new member needs two things: a row in the membership state, and the **scope key** to actually read the scope's encrypted operations. The key is delivered privately, wrapped to the joiner's public key (ECDH), by existing members who already hold it.

The membership operation is public on the governance scope; the key delivery is a private, wrapped envelope. Once the joiner unwraps the key, every prior and future operation in the scope becomes readable, and they fold the scope just like anyone else.

### Which group an invitation may target

An invitation names a `group_id`, and that may be the namespace root or a `Restricted` subgroup — but **not a subgroup on an `Open` chain to its namespace**. The reason is the key, not the membership row.

An Open-chain subgroup is encrypted under the **namespace** key ([Which group's key covers a group](/protocol/encryption/#which-groups-key-covers-a-group)), so the only key that would let such a joiner read the group it was invited to is the namespace key — and that key reads all namespace-level governance and every Open-chain subgroup's application state, retained after leaving unless rotated. A member admitted to one subgroup is not entitled to it. There is therefore no key the join can be answered with, and the responder refuses the invitation rather than admitting a member who could read nothing.

The route in is an invitation to the **namespace root**. From there, inheritance carries the member into the Open subgroup for free: parent-group members holding `CAN_JOIN_OPEN_SUBGROUPS` are inherited as members, and the self-join publishes `MemberJoinedOpen` — which needs no key delivery of its own, because the namespace key the member already holds covers the subgroup.


## Membership: leaving and removal

There are two ways a member stops being a member, and the difference is entirely about the **scope key**.

| Path | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Leave (self-initiated)** | A member removes themselves. No key rotation happens — the leaver already holds the key and could have copied it, so rotating would be theater. Leaving is just a membership operation that drops their own row. |
| **Removal (admin-initiated)** | An admin removes someone else. Here the key **is** rotated: a fresh scope key is generated and delivered, wrapped, to every _remaining_ member — so the removed member can read nothing authored after their removal. This is forward secrecy. |

Two invariants guard both paths: the scope's **owner** cannot be removed involuntarily and cannot self-leave without first transferring ownership, and a removal that would leave a group with no admin is rejected — the tree can never be orphaned.

### Worked example: promote a member, then remove one

Two operations, both signed by an admin, show how a role change and a removal compose. First promote Bob to admin with `MemberRoleSet { member, role: Admin }` (the upsert-role op); later remove Carol with `MemberRemoved`. Promotion is a plain membership write — no key changes. Removal is admin-initiated, so it carries a fresh scope key wrapped to every _remaining_ member (Bob now included, Carol excluded), the `KeyRotation { new_key_id, envelopes }` bundle attached to the op.

## Auto-follow: turning membership into replication

Membership is a row in governance state; it is not, by itself, replicated data. Auto-follow is the mechanism that closes that gap — it is the answer to "I joined the group but no data synced." A member row carries two **auto-follow flags**, and when they are set the node automatically joins the contexts (and, eventually, subgroups) it is already entitled to, so membership turns into an actual replicating context on disk.

The flags are stored per member, per group:

| Flag | Default | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `contexts` | `true` | Auto-join every context registered in this group. |
| `subgroups` | `false` | Auto-join subgroups this node is entitled to. |

`contexts` defaults to `true` because joining a group implies wanting the group's data — the user's mental model is "I'm in this namespace, I want its contexts." Opting out is an explicit per-member, per-group set-auto-follow operation flipping `contexts` to `false`. `subgroups` defaults to `false` and is currently inert for ordinary roles (it requires an admission op that does not yet exist); the TEE fleet-join path is the one place that sets both flags, via the replica's own self-signed op after admission.

### Event-driven join

Auto-follow is purely event-driven — there is no startup re-scan. When a `ContextRegistered` op applies in a group, the handler asks one question: is this node a member of that group with `auto_follow.contexts = true` (and has it not opted out — see below)? If so, it emits a `JoinContext` for the new context. That single decision point is what keeps a member's replica set in step with the group as new contexts appear.

### Backfill when the flag flips on

Event-driven join only covers contexts that register _after_ the flag is on. To cover contexts that already exist when `auto_follow.contexts` flips to `true`, flipping the flag synthesizes an `AutoFollowSet` event: the handler enumerates the group's existing contexts and joins any it has not already joined. This backfill is a **single pass** capped at `BACKFILL_LIMIT = 1000` contexts. If a group holds more than that at flip-time, the remainder are picked up event-driven as their `ContextRegistered` ops continue to apply, or by re-flipping the flag.

### The fresh-joiner cascade

The implicit trigger is what makes a brand-new member backfill contexts that existed _before_ it joined. The auto-follow handler does not subscribe to `MemberAdded` at all — it subscribes to `AutoFollowSet`. So when a member is added with `auto_follow.contexts` set (the default), the `MemberAdded` apply site synthesizes an `AutoFollowSet` event for that member. This routes a fresh joiner through the exact same backfill cascade as an explicit flag flip, closing the regression where the UI showed group membership but no data synced. The handler short-circuits as "not for self" on every node except the joiner, so the cascade fires exactly once per membership change.

### Inherited members: the anchor rule

A node need not hold a direct member row in a group to auto-follow it. In an Open subgroup, membership flows down by inheritance from an ancestor that granted `CAN_JOIN_OPEN_SUBGROUPS` (the [capability inheritance](/protocol/capability-inheritance/) model). When a node has no direct row in the group, auto-follow walks up the Open-visibility chain to the **anchor** — the nearest ancestor where the node holds a real direct row — and honours _that_ row's `auto_follow.contexts` flag. This is depth-independent: the anchor is always a genuine direct-row member, so its flag is authoritative.

This rule is what lets a root-admitted TEE (`ReadOnlyTee` or `RelayTee`) replicate Open-subgroup contexts. The TEE replica holds only a root row, so for every Open subgroup beneath the root the anchor resolves to the namespace root, and the root row's flag drives replication into subgroups where the replica was never explicitly admitted.

### Opting out of a single context

Auto-follow is a group-level intent, but a node can suppress it for one context. Calling `leave_context` writes a node-local `ContextLeftMarker` for `(member, context)` into a local-only column — it is **never synced to peers**. While that marker is present, auto-follow skips the context even though `auto_follow.contexts` is still `true` at the group level, so a later `ContextRegistered` (or backfill) will not silently rejoin it. The marker is cleared only by an explicit user-initiated join request for that context.

## Startup self-heal

Governance apply is online and event-driven, but two crash/race windows can leave a node holding work that no future event will re-trigger. Both are swept once, on the way up.

### Re-driving stranded buffered ops

A governance op a node receives before it holds the scope key is **buffered** awaiting that key. The race: the op (e.g. a `ContextRegistered`) arrives and buffers, `GroupCreated` applies, the live one-shot `KeyDelivery` retry then runs and _fails_ (the key has not landed yet), and nothing remains to re-drive the buffered op. On startup the node sweeps every namespace it holds an identity for, and for every group whose key it **already holds** that still has buffered ops, it re-drives them (`crates/context/src/self_purge.rs`).

The sweep is a **fixpoint loop**, because applying one op can unblock a later op from a _different_ signer (the nonce window is tracked per `(group, signer)`, so one pass orders only within a signer). It re-runs until a full pass applies nothing new — narrowing each pass to only the groups that just made progress — bounded by `MAX_PASSES = 64` as a defensive cap against a logic bug. It runs before the event loop and after the op-event subscription, so any `ContextRegistered` events it emits drive [auto-follow](#auto-follow-turning-membership-into-replication) rather than being lost. It is best-effort: per-group errors are logged and swallowed, and the next restart re-runs it.

### Re-spawning interrupted upgrades

A [group upgrade](/protocol/upgrades/) is driven by an in-memory propagator; a crash mid-walk leaves the upgrade row `InProgress` with no task pushing it. On actor startup the `ContextManager` scans the store for in-progress upgrades and re-spawns a propagator for each (`recover_in_progress_upgrades`, `crates/context/src/lifecycle.rs`). Recovery is unconditional: a cascade descendant left `InProgress` by a crash would otherwise block that group's next upgrade forever.

## Roles, capabilities, and the owner

Authority is layered. A member has a **role** for coarse rights, and fine-grained **capabilities** for specific actions; the **owner** is a distinguished identity sitting above roles.

| Role | Can do |
| --- | --- |
| `Admin` | All governance operations: manage members, set policy and capabilities, manage subgroups. |
| `Member` | Read and write context state. Governance actions only with the matching capability. |
| `ReadOnly` | Read state; writes are rejected, locally and on peers. Still a replica: it applies the deltas other members author. |
| `ReadOnlyTee` | A TEE replica: a read-only fleet node admitted by hardware attestation; evicted with a self-purge signal. Never relays members' writes. |
| `RelayTee` | A TEE relay: everything `ReadOnlyTee` is, and it may author members' writes under their signed warrants. Admitted when the namespace's TEE admission mode is `relay`. |

A member's role in a group is its **effective** role: its own row there, or, when it reaches an Open subgroup only by inheritance, the role of the ancestor row it inherits through. A member that is read-only at the namespace root is read-only in every Open-subgroup context it inherits into, for its own writes locally and on every peer; see [the role of an inherited member](/protocol/capability-inheritance/#the-role-of-an-inherited-member).

A TEE row changes role only between `ReadOnlyTee` and `RelayTee`, under the admission policy's mode; a role change, re-add or admin handoff that would make an attested TEE a `Member`, `ReadOnly` or `Admin` is refused (`TeeMemberRoleLocked`). Remove the TEE instead. See [TEE attestation](/protocol/tee-attestation/#the-tee-roles-readonlytee-and-relaytee).

**Capabilities** are individual grants a non-admin can hold — for example: create contexts, invite members, join open subgroups, manage members, manage the application, or create/delete/configure subgroups. Admins pass every capability check implicitly; defaults can be set per group and overridden per member.

The **owner** is not a role but a stored identity with exclusive rights — transfer ownership, delete the group or namespace — and the removal immunity noted above. Every other admin is a regular admin.

## Authorization: judged at the author's cut

The decision "was this author allowed to write this operation?" reuses the primitive from [Projection](/protocol/projection/): the projection resolves membership and capabilities at the operation's **causal cut** — the governance state the author had observed — not at the receiver's present.

:::note[The rule]
**Forward-only authority.**

An operation is authorized against the membership and access-control state that existed at its parents. So a write authored while you were still a member stays valid even if a peer applies your removal first; and a governance action you took as admin stands even if you were later demoted. Every node reaches the same verdict because every node folds the same cut — authority converges for free, with no coordination.
:::

The same mechanism covers data writes: an entity's **writer set** (the access-control plane) is resolved at the cut, and a write is accepted only if its author was an authorized writer at that point.

### A removal voids the signer's concurrent governance ops

Resolving authority at the cut alone would let a removed admin keep acting: an op it signs against a cut from before its removal is authorized at that cut, on every node, however late it arrives. So a **governance** op is also void when the log holds a removal of its signer that is neither its ancestor nor its descendant. The removal can be a member removal, a demotion from admin, the revocation of the device that signed the op, or the revocation of a capability the op relied on. Once a node knows of the removal, no op of that signer concurrent with it carries authority, and every node computes the same answer from the same log in any arrival order.

- A device revocation counts as a removal only if its author was an admin of the root group at its own cut. The apply logs a revocation it refused, so one from anyone else removes nothing. A self-service revocation rests on a root-signed proof that the log does not carry, and a subgroup admin's ejection is scoped to that subgroup, so neither voids the device's concurrent ops.
- A role change counts as a demotion only if its author was an admin too.
- A capability set counts as a revocation of the bits the member held at its cut and does not keep, only if its author was an admin of the group at that cut. It voids only the member's concurrent ops in that group that needed one of those bits: an op a non-admin needs the bit for (adding or removing members for `MANAGE_MEMBERS`, metadata for `CAN_MANAGE_METADATA`, an application target, migration or upgrade for `MANAGE_APPLICATION`, a context registration for `CAN_CREATE_CONTEXT`, a subgroup's creation, deletion or visibility for its own bit), made when the member was not an admin at the op's cut. An op that needed no removed bit stands, as does any op of a member who was an admin at its cut. A grant voids nothing. Capabilities are not inherited, so the revocation reaches only the group it names.
- Ops before the removal stand. Ops after it are refused by the cut check, as before.
- A void op is still logged and still part of the causal graph, so descendants keep their parents. It is not applied, its nonce is spent, and a key rotation riding on it is not taken. A node keeps only so many bytes of such ops per signer and per namespace; past that it keeps an op's place in the log and not its contents, so the DAG still moves on. A void op keeps what the rule reads of it (only the bulk of a policy is dropped), so every node still reads the same void set. For an op the node cannot read the cost is real: the contents cannot be replayed when the key arrives, and authority questions about that group stay undecided on that node.
- Ops by other members stay valid, including ones concurrent with the removal.
- The void cascades. An admin a void op added, a capability it granted and a rotation it made are recomputed without it, so what that admin then did is void too.
- Two admins who remove each other concurrently are both removed. A cycle of three or more such removals voids every one of them, and nobody is removed. The namespace owner's ops are never void, and the owner cannot be removed. The exemption covers any op of the removed admin that removes or demotes the account that removed it, whichever group the op acts in.
- A removal that is itself void voids nothing.
- Judging whether a signer still held its authority without a void grant costs a fold of its history. The work is bounded, and past the bound the remaining candidates are void, the same on every node.
- When a removal arrives after a node already applied a concurrent op of the same signer, the node rebuilds the affected group's members, roles, capabilities and current group key from the log without the void ops. A removal that itself becomes void later puts back what it had voided. Other effects such an op had (for example group metadata or an app target) are not undone, only withheld from a node that hears of the removal first.
- A default capability or visibility change made by a void op is left out of every view, but ops that relied on it are not revisited.
- A node judges with the ops it can read. A removal sealed under a key the node lacks is not seen until the key arrives, and the ops parked behind it are judged again then.

Two consequences are worth telling users. An admin removed while offline loses the governance edits it made offline, and a client should say so before an admin works offline. And data sealed under a group key that a removed admin rotated to stays readable by that admin: the key is kept so that what peers sealed under it before they heard of the removal still opens, it is never the current key again, and it cannot be recalled.

What the rule does not reach: a change of the namespace admin or owner that a void op made before the node heard of the removal is not undone (and the owner the rule exempts moves with it), a TEE removal a node applied has already purged it, an invitation signed by an admin before its removal still admits the joiner, because the inviter is not part of the logged join; and an op sent on an admin's behalf through a relay is attributed to the relay. Both need the inviter or the warrant's author in the logged op.

This rule is part of `SignedNamespaceOp` version 21: a node on an older version applies such an op, so nodes of one namespace must be upgraded together.

## Shared writer-set rotations

A `SharedStorage` cell's writer set changes by a governance op, `GroupOp::SharedWritersRotated { context_id, cell, prior, nonce, new }`, published encrypted in the group that owns the context.
Its place in the causal order is the signed op's own, so every node that folds the same governance cut reaches the same writer set, however the op reached it.

The apply checks only what the op and its signer's standing at the op's cut decide, and writes only a record that the context has rotated cells:

- `cell` is a cell id, and `new` and `prior` are not empty and hold at most 256 accounts each (checked on the apply, since a decrypted group op is not validated on the way in);
- the signing key speaks for an account that is a member of the group, not read-only there, and not a TEE of the namespace (a TEE-triggered run signs with the TEE's key);
- that account holds `ADMIN` in `prior`.

An op naming a context that is not registered in the op's group has no effect, so the order of a concurrent detach cannot make nodes disagree.
The op is not delegable, so a relay cannot publish it for a member.

Which steps take effect is a fold over the cell's steps in the cut's ancestry (`calimero_storage::shared_writers::fold`), ordered by the governance DAG, never by clock.
Only steps published in the context's group count, each only when its signer stood at the step's own parents as the apply requires: the key speaks for an account there, that account is a member of the group and not read-only, and it is not a TEE of the namespace.
A node that got the group's key late therefore folds the same steps as every other node.
A step whose op is void, because its signer was removed or demoted concurrently (see above), does not count either. A rotation needs no member capability, so a capability revoke does not void it.
Only steps that rest on the genesis set count, that is a step from it or from a set another such step left, signed by an admin of the set it steps from.
A cell folds at most 256 such steps (`MAX_STEPS_PER_CELL`), which bounds the cost of a cut: past that it has no answer, so writes to it wait, rather than a truncated history rolling a rotation back. That is a lifetime budget of rotations for a cell until governance history is checkpointed, and an admin of the cell, a removed one included, can spend it by publishing steps.

1. It starts from the set the cell id commits to, found among the steps' `prior` sets; no step can supply a genesis of its own.
2. A step counts only when its `prior` is the cell's set in effect in the step's own causal past, and its author holds `ADMIN` in that set.
3. A step is void when a concurrent step (neither is in the other's causal past) by another account removes its author's `ADMIN`, however low its nonce.
   It is also void when the step that granted its author `ADMIN`, directly or through earlier grants, is void, so an admin added on a void branch acts for nothing.
   An admin removed by a rotation therefore cannot override it by forking from any earlier cut, directly or through an admin its fork adds.
4. Two concurrent steps race when each removes an admin the other's grants rest on.
   In a race, a step that removes an admin of the cell's genesis set (the set its id commits to) does not apply, so a genesis admin keeps `ADMIN` through any race; removing one outside a race, including by a step that has seen its steps, is still allowed.
   The other removals of a race all take effect: each removed admin keeps only what its remover's step left it, nothing else from either step applies, and steps that have seen both build on that set.
   If the racing admins were the cell's only ones, it keeps its other writers and can no longer be rotated.
   Of a cycle of three or more concurrent removals every step is void and nobody is removed.
5. From genesis, the fold follows the live step built on the set in effect.
   Steps built on one set are concurrent, and of those the lowest `(nonce, signer key, op id)` wins: the nonce is only that tie-break, so an author's sequential rotations apply in causal order whatever its clock says.

`ScopeProjections::shared_writers_at_cut` answers at a governance cut, and only when the cut's ancestry is complete and every op of the context's group, its ancestors and the namespace root in it is readable.
A context whose cells have rotated cannot be detached from its group (`HasRotatedCells`), judged at the op's own cut, so its rotations stay with the group readers fold them from.
Registering it in another group is refused only from this node's own record: a rotation published in a group whose key a node lacks cannot be seen at the cut, so the cut alone would split nodes.
Only a step from a cell's genesis set, signed by a member in standing who is an admin of it, published in the context's group, marks a context as rotated.
A member can make a cell of their own in a context, so any member can mark a context this way, and an admin then cannot detach or delete it; removing the member does not undo it.
Deleting the group unregisters its contexts, and a context outside any group has no writer set to read.

Two limits follow from the rules above.
A genesis admin removed by another can answer with a concurrent step removing the remover, and then both keep ADMIN, so one genesis admin cannot reliably remove another.
And a step's standing check reads the namespace root admin and its default capabilities as this node holds them, as every other at-cut check does.

Pruning governance history must keep every cell's rotation steps, or a folded checkpoint of each cell's writer set and the cut it holds at.
A cut that reaches a pruned prefix without one has no answer (`None`), never the genesis set.
Execution does not publish these ops yet; until it does, a cell's writers are resolved from its storage rotation log.

## The algorithm: authorization at the cut

Below is the whole security model in two folds — the governance plane and the data plane — written to match the code, so it is reimplementation-grade. Both resolve authority at the **cut the op references**, never at the receiver's present.

The governance plane is a single fold (`authorize`) over the op's payload, decided against an `AclView` the projection resolves at the op's causal parents (`acl_view_at(op.parents)`). It is the one causal-auth boundary; it never walks the DAG itself.

```text
// view = ScopeState::acl_view_at(op.parents)
//        -> membership + capabilities + per-object ACL, as of the op's causal cut
fn authorize(op, view) -> Result<(), Rejected>:
    match op.payload:
        Put{entity}          => require view.may(op.author, entity, WRITE)    // else NotPermitted
        Delete{entity}       => require view.may(op.author, entity, DELETE)   // else NotPermitted
        SetWriters{object}   => require view.is_owner(op.author, object)      // = may(.., ADMIN); else NotOwner
        SharedWritersRotated{prior} => require prior[op.author].contains(ADMIN)  // else NotOwner
        MemberAdded|MemberRemoved{group}                  => require view.is_group_admin(op.author, group)  // else NotGroupAdmin
        SubgroupVisibilitySet{scope}                      => require view.is_group_admin(op.author, scope)
        DefaultCapabilitiesSet|MemberCapabilitySet{group} => require view.is_group_admin(op.author, group)
        AdminChanged|PolicyUpdated|SubgroupCreated|SubgroupReparented|SubgroupDeleted
                             => require view.is_root_admin(op.author)         // else NotRootAdmin
        Noop                 => Ok

// The writer-set check (default-write = membership), two-tier:
fn may(author, entity, required: OpMask) -> bool:
    if let Some(writers) = view.acl.get(entity):    // restricted object: explicit ACL is authoritative
        return writers.get(author).is_some_and(|held| held.contains(required))
    // non-restricted: any scope member implicitly holds WRITE|DELETE — but NOT ADMIN,
    // so a plain member can write/delete default data yet cannot rotate its writer set.
    return view.is_scope_member(author) and (WRITE|DELETE).contains(required)
```

Note what the fold actually keys on: governance ops are gated by **role at the cut** (group admin, or root admin for namespace-level ops), and the `u32` capability bitmask gates membership inheritance (the `CAN_JOIN_OPEN_SUBGROUPS` bit on the open-subgroup walk), not these admin ops. A capability is read as `view.capability(group, member)` = explicit per-member override, else the group default, else `0`, then bit-tested.

The data plane (`apply_action`) is the writer-set analogue: it resolves the authoritative writer set per `StorageType` and verifies a **per-action signature** against it, then enforces the operation's required bit. The writer set is resolved at the **delta's own causal parents** (`effective_writers = writers_at(delta.parents)`), the data-DAG cut, while the delta separately carries a `governance_position` (the governance heads at sign time) so receivers gate membership at the governance cut too.

```text
fn apply_action(action, ctx):
    required = required_op_mask(action)         // Add|Update => WRITE, DeleteRef => DELETE
    match action.storage_type:
        Public  => accept                       // no signature; membership is the only gate
        Frozen  => verify content hash          // immutable, content-addressed (no signature)
        User{owner} =>
            require ed25519_verify(sig, owner, payload_for_signing)   // exactly one writer: the owner
        Shared{writers} | SharedMember{anchor} =>
            set    = ctx.effective_writers ?? stored_writers          // writers at the delta's cut
            signer = set.keys().find(|w| ed25519_verify(sig, w, payload_for_signing))
            require signer.is_some()                                  // else InvalidSignature
            require set[signer].contains(required)                   // op-granularity bit; else ActionNotAllowed
```

**Why the cut, not "now":** because every input is resolved at the op's referenced causal position, the verdict is a pure function of state the author had already observed. Two nodes that fold the same DAG reach the same answer regardless of receive order, so authority converges with no coordination. And it is forward-only: a write authored before a revocation stays valid even if a peer applies the revocation first — the revocation is not in the write's causal past, so it cannot retroactively un-authorize it.

## Specification

### Capability bits

Capabilities are a `u32` bitmask. Each grant is a fixed bit position:

| Bit | Value | Capability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `0x0001` | `CAN_CREATE_CONTEXT` |
| 1 | `0x0002` | `CAN_INVITE_MEMBERS` |
| 2 | `0x0004` | `CAN_JOIN_OPEN_SUBGROUPS` |
| 3 | `0x0008` | `MANAGE_MEMBERS` |
| 4 | `0x0010` | `MANAGE_APPLICATION` |
| 5 | `0x0020` | `CAN_CREATE_SUBGROUP` |
| 6 | `0x0040` | `CAN_DELETE_SUBGROUP` |
| 7 | `0x0080` | `CAN_MANAGE_VISIBILITY` |
| 8 | `0x0100` | `CAN_MANAGE_METADATA` |

Roles are a distinct enum: `Admin`, `Member`, `ReadOnly`, `ReadOnlyTee`, `RelayTee`. Admins pass every capability check implicitly.

### The signed governance op

Governance operations travel as a signed envelope. The group-scoped form:

```rust
struct SignedGroupOp {
  version: u8,  // current schema version = 15; checked strictly-equal on decode
  group_id: [u8; 32],
  parent_op_hashes: Vec<[u8; 32]>,  // DAG parents
  signer: PublicKey,
  nonce: u64,  // per-signer monotonic, starts at 1
  op: GroupOp,
  signature: [u8; 64],
}
```

The content hash (and the bytes the signature covers) are **domain-separated**:

```text
op_id = SHA-256( DOMAIN ‖ borsh(signable) )  // signable = all fields except signature
signature = Ed25519_sign( signer_secret, DOMAIN ‖ borsh(signable) )

// group ops     DOMAIN = b"calimero.group.v1"
// namespace ops DOMAIN = b"calimero.namespace.v1"  (SignedNamespaceOp, version = 22)
```

### Replay protection

Each _(group, signer)_ tracks a **nonce window**: a contiguous applied `floor` plus a sparse set of out-of-order applied nonces above it. A nonce is a duplicate if `nonce ≤ floor` or it is already in the set. This is checked _before_ apply, and tolerates concurrent siblings (same author, different parents) arriving out of order without false-flagging them. Nonces start at `1`; `0` is never issued.

### Key delivery: the wrapped envelope

A scope key is delivered wrapped per recipient:

```rust
struct KeyEnvelope {
  recipient: EnvelopeRecipient,  // a member identity OR one device, + its ECDH key
  sender: PublicKey,             // authenticated by `signature`
  nonce: [u8; 12],  // AES-GCM nonce
  ciphertext: Vec<u8>,  // AES-256-GCM(scope_key)
  signature: [u8; 64],  // Ed25519 by `sender`, bound to the group
}
```

- **Addressing** — `EnvelopeRecipient::Member { identity, ephemeral_pk }` or `Device { device, ephemeral_pk }`. The variant decides the agreement, so the two are one field. Member addressing is the bootstrap form and is permanent. A *joiner* is bound the moment it is a member — its join op carries the credential in the clear — so the device-addressed form covers the ordinary case. What still needs member addressing is every enrolment that is **not** a join: `AccountDeviceLinked` is an *encrypted* group op, so a node must already hold the scope key to publish one, which is exactly the position a re-admitted member or one whose devices are all revoked is in.
- **Shared secret** — a fresh ephemeral keypair per envelope (that is the forward secrecy), agreed against the recipient's key: ECDH on the Curve25519 form of the Ed25519 identity for a member, native X25519 for a device. The raw point is HKDF-derived into the AES-256 key, under a per-scheme label so the two agreements can never collide.
- **AEAD** — AES-256-GCM, 12-byte nonce, empty additional data, GCM tag appended inline.
- **Authentication** — `sender` signs the canonical envelope bytes bound to the `group_id`, checked *before* any ECDH, so a forged or cross-group-replayed envelope is rejected first. The addressing variant tag is inside those bytes, so the two modes cannot be swapped.
- **Rotation** — a removal carries a `KeyRotation { new_key_id = SHA-256(new_key), envelopes }` with one envelope per _remaining_ recipient; the removed member and every device of theirs get none. One bundle may mix both addressing modes.

A scope key is identified on the wire by `key_id = SHA-256(scope_key)`, so a receiver can look up which delivered key decrypts a given operation.

### Owner vs admin

The owner is stored alongside admin in the group metadata (`owner_identity` vs `admin_identity`). A group may have several admins but exactly one owner. Only the owner may sign `TransferOwnership` or delete the group/namespace, and the owner is immune to involuntary removal. Ownership is itself folded into the governance hash, so an op signed before vs after a transfer resolves against different state.
